Section 14.1 • Estate Definition
Foundation

What Constitutes an "Estate"?

In legal and financial planning terminology, an Estate refers to the total net worth of an individual at any given point in time or upon demise. It encompasses all assets—movable and immovable, tangible and intangible, physical and digital—owned solely or jointly by the individual, reduced by all legal debts, liabilities, and obligations.

Fundamental Net Estate Equation:
Net Distributable Estate = Total Gross Assets − (Secured Debts + Unsecured Liabilities + Outstanding Tax Dues + Funeral & Administration Expenses)
Immovable Real Estate

Freehold land, residential flats, commercial properties, agricultural plots, undivided shares in common amenities, leasehold rights.

Financial Assets

Bank savings/term deposits, mutual fund units, demat equities, corporate bonds, government securities, PPF, EPF, and insurance maturity/death proceeds.

Business Interests

Sole proprietorship capital, partnership firm capital and share in profits/goodwill, LLP partner rights, unlisted/listed shares in private limited companies.

Tangible Movables

Gold bullion, silver, family jewellery, vintage artifacts, artwork, luxury motor vehicles, household furnishings, safe deposit locker contents.

Digital & IP Assets

Cryptocurrency wallets, non-fungible tokens (NFTs), patents, trademarks, software copyrights, book royalties, monetized digital channels.

Liabilities & Deductions

Housing loans, personal loans, business overdrafts, unpaid direct taxes (ITD claims), pending consumer or civil litigation awards, estate administration costs.

Section 14.2 • Modes of Succession
Core Legal Distinction

Testamentary vs. Intestate Succession

Succession is the legal process through which the property of a deceased person is transferred to living persons. The entire framework of succession hinges on whether the deceased left behind a legally binding declaration of intent:

Testamentary Succession

With Valid Will

Occurs when an individual (the Testator) dies having executed a legally valid Will or Codicil disposing of their self-acquired assets. The distribution follows the testator's instructions, overriding default statutory succession laws.

  • Complete autonomy over asset division.
  • Allows appointment of trusted Executors.
  • Can provide for minors through testamentary guardians.

Intestate Succession

Without Will

Occurs when an individual dies without executing a valid Will, or where the Will is found invalid/void. The estate (the deceased is called the Intestate) devolves automatically according to personal religious succession laws.

  • Deceased's personal wishes are completely irrelevant.
  • Rigid statutory division across defined classes of heirs.
  • Requires court-issued Succession Certificate or Letters of Administration.
Section 14.3 • Nature of Property
⭐ High Exam Yield

Ancestral Property vs. Self-Acquired Property

Under Hindu Law, the distinction between self-acquired property and ancestral property is pivotal because it dictates whether an individual can freely alienate the property through a Will or gift:

Criterion Self-Acquired Property Ancestral Property (Coparcenary)
Definition Acquired through own earnings, personal investments, gifts from non-ancestors, or unencumbered testamentary bequest. Inherited undivided up to four generations of male lineage (father, grandfather, great-grandfather).
Right by Birth No child or heir acquires any right by birth. Absolute ownership rests with the acquirer during their lifetime. Every coparcener acquires an equal undivided share by birth automatically.
Power of Alienation (Gift / Will) Full, unrestricted power to sell, gift, mortgage, or bequeath via Will to anyone, completely disinheriting children. Cannot be gifted or bequeathed through Will to the exclusion of other coparceners without their explicit consent (except undivided notional share under Sec 30).
Daughter's Status Post-2005 Daughter inherits as Class I heir if parent dies intestate. Parent can choose not to bequeath in Will. Daughter is a Coparcener by birth with identical rights and liabilities as a son (HSA Amendment 2005).
Landmark Supreme Court Ruling: Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020)

The Supreme Court of India held that daughters have coparcenary rights by birth under Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (as amended in 2005). It is irrelevant whether the father was alive on September 9, 2005 (the date of amendment). The right is conferred by birth and has retroactive effect, settling years of conflicting High Court precedents.

Section 14.4 • Hindu Succession Act, 1956
⭐ ULTRA HIGH YIELD

Male Intestate Succession (Section 8)

When a Hindu male dies intestate, his property devolves strictly according to Section 8 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 in the following cascading order of priority:

Tier 1 (Simultaneous) Class I Heirs Takes priority over all other tiers
Tier 2 (If No Class I) Class II Heirs Category I excludes Category II
Tier 3 (If No Class II) Agnates Wholly male bloodline
Tier 4 (If No Agnates) Cognates Bloodline with female link

Class I Heirs (Simultaneous & Equal Inheritors)

Critical: Father is NOT Class I!

Class I heirs inherit simultaneously and to the total exclusion of all Class II heirs. The primary Class I heirs are:

• Son
• Daughter
• Widow
• Mother ⭐
Branch Heirs of Predeceased Children: Son of predeceased son, Daughter of predeceased son, Widow of predeceased son; Son of predeceased daughter, Daughter of predeceased daughter; Son of predeceased son of predeceased son, Daughter of predeceased son of predeceased son, Widow of predeceased son of predeceased son; and descendants of predeceased daughters added in 2005.

Class II Heirs (Ordered by Nine Categories)

Class II heirs only inherit if there is NOT A SINGLE Class I heir alive. Among Class II heirs, Category I excludes Category II, Category II excludes Category III, and so on:

Category I: Father (Takes 100% if no Class I heirs; completely excludes Categories II to IX)
Category II: (1) Son's daughter's son, (2) Son's daughter's daughter, (3) Brother, (4) Sister (Divide equally)
Category III: Daughter's son's son, Daughter's son's daughter, Daughter's daughter's son, Daughter's daughter's daughter
Category IV: Brother's son, Sister's son, Brother's daughter, Sister's daughter (Nephews and Nieces)
Categories V–IX: Paternal & Maternal Grandparents, Widows of Father/Brother, Uncles and Aunts (Paternal then Maternal)
Practical Level 2 Calculation Example:

Scenario: Ramesh, a Hindu male, dies intestate leaving a self-acquired estate of ₹50 Lakhs. He is survived by his Mother, his Widow, 1 Son, 2 Daughters, and his Father. How will his estate be distributed?

1. Father gets ₹0 because Father is a Class II heir, and the presence of Class I heirs completely excludes Class II.

2. Class I Heirs alive: Mother (1 share), Widow (1 share), Son (1 share), Daughter 1 (1 share), Daughter 2 (1 share) = Total 5 equal shares.

Each of the 5 Class I heirs receives ₹10 Lakhs (1/5th share each).

Section 14.5 • Hindu Succession Act, 1956
⭐ ULTRA HIGH YIELD EXAM TRAP

Female Intestate Succession (Sections 15 & 16)

The rules governing the devolution of property of a female Hindu dying intestate are fundamentally different from those applicable to males. They are governed by Section 15 of the Act:

General Rule of Devolution (Section 15(1))

Applies to property acquired by the female by her own skill, purchase, gift from non-parents, or general investments:

1st Priority Sons, Daughters (including predeceased children's issues) & Husband
2nd Priority Heirs of the Husband
3rd Priority Mother and Father
4th Priority Heirs of the Father
5th Priority Heirs of the Mother

Special Reversion Exceptions (Section 15(2)) — HEAVILY TESTED

When a female Hindu dies without leaving any son or daughter (or children of predeceased son/daughter):

Rule 1: Property Inherited from Father or Mother (Sec 15(2)(a))

If the property was inherited from her father or mother, and she dies childless, the property does NOT go to her husband or husband's heirs! Instead, it devolves upon the heirs of her father.

Example: Childless female dies leaving husband and brother. Property inherited from father goes 100% to brother (father's heir). Husband gets ₹0!
Rule 2: Property Inherited from Husband or Father-in-Law (Sec 15(2)(b))

If the property was inherited from her husband or father-in-law, and she dies childless, the property does NOT go to her own parents or siblings! Instead, it devolves upon the heirs of the husband.

Example: Widow dies childless leaving her mother and husband's nephew. Property inherited from husband goes 100% to husband's nephew. Mother gets ₹0!
Section 14.6 • Other Succession Frameworks
Comparative Law

Indian Succession Act, 1925 & Personal Laws

The Indian Succession Act, 1925 (ISA) serves two distinct functions: it governs testamentary succession (Wills) uniformly across most communities, and it provides default intestate succession rules for Christians, Jews, and Parsis:

Christian Intestate Rules

• With Children: Widow gets 1/3rd share, remaining 2/3rd share is divided equally among children.
• No Children, with Kindred: Widow gets 1/2 share, kindred get 1/2 share.
• No Kindred: Widow takes 100% of the estate.

Parsi Intestate Rules

Governed by Sections 50 to 56 of the ISA.
• Widow/husband and each child receive equal shares.
• Each parent receives a share equal to half the share of each child.

Muslim Law (Shariat)

• Intestate succession is divided strictly among fixed Quranic Sharers and Residuaries.
• ⭐ Testamentary Restriction: A Muslim cannot bequeath more than 1/3rd of their net estate by Will without the express consent of all legal heirs.

Special Marriage Act, 1954 (SMA) Application:

Under Section 21 of the SMA, succession to the property of persons married under the SMA is governed by the Indian Succession Act, 1925. However, by virtue of the 1976 amendment (Section 21A), if both parties to the marriage are Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs, or Jains, the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 continues to apply to them.

Section 14.7 • Real Estate Records
⭐ Critical Legal Distinction

Mutation of Property vs. Legal Title

One of the most frequent misconceptions in Indian estate planning is confusing Mutation (Dakhil Kharij / Namantaran) with legal ownership. In real estate advisory, clear appreciation of this distinction is paramount:

What Mutation IS NOT
  • • Mutation does NOT create or extinguish legal title.
  • • Mutation entry is NOT a document of ownership.
  • • Having one's name in municipal / land revenue records does NOT defeat the legitimate claims of co-owners or legal heirs.
What Mutation ACTUALLY IS
  • • Purely an administrative recording for fiscal / revenue collection.
  • • Enables the municipal corporation or Panchayat to levy and collect property tax from the designated person.
  • • Legal title is derived only through registered sale deeds, registered gift deeds, probate, or statutory succession.
Supreme Court Jurisprudence: Bhimabai Mahadeo Kambekar (2021):

The Supreme Court has consistently held across dozens of authoritative rulings that: "Mutation of property in revenue records neither creates nor extinguishes title to the property, nor has it any presumptive value on title. Such entries are relevant only for the purpose of paying property tax and land revenue."
