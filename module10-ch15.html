Section 15.1 • Testamentary Tools
⭐ High Exam Yield

Wills: Legal Definition, Execution & Attestation

Under Section 2(h) of the Indian Succession Act, 1925, a Will is defined as: "the legal declaration of the intention of a testator with respect to his property which he desires to be carried into effect after his death." It is an ambulatory, revocable instrument that operates strictly post-mortem.

Sound Disposing Mind

Testator must be an adult (18+) possessing full testamentary capacity. A person of unsound mind can execute during a lucid interval.

Ambulatory & Revocable

Has zero legal effect during the testator's lifetime. Can be modified, updated, or revoked at will any number of times.

Free & Voluntary

Must be free from fraud, coercion, or undue influence (Section 61 ISA). Any clause procured by coercion is void.

Mandatory Formalities of Execution (Section 63 ISA)

  • Signature / Mark: Testator must sign or affix their mark (or have another person sign in their presence and direction).
  • Minimum Two Attesting Witnesses: The Will MUST be attested by at least two witnesses, each of whom must have seen the testator sign or received personal acknowledgment.
  • Knowledge of Contents NOT Required: Witnesses do not need to know the contents of the Will; they only attest that the testator signed it in their presence.
⭐ High-Yield Rule (Section 67 ISA): A bequest to an attesting witness (or their spouse) is VOID. The Will remains valid, but the attesting witness receives nothing. (Best advisory practice: Always choose completely neutral, non-beneficiary witnesses).
Registration of Will

Registration under the Indian Registration Act, 1908 is purely optional. An unregistered Will is completely valid in law. However, registration before the Sub-Registrar provides strong evidentiary backing against allegations of forgery or mental incapacity.

The Residuary Clause

Every professional Will must contain a Residuary Clause disposing of all remaining assets not explicitly enumerated (including future acquired assets). In its absence, omitted assets devolve via intestate succession.

Section 15.2 • Administration
⭐ Heavily Tested

Codicil, Revocation, Probate & Court Grants

Codicil (Section 2(b) ISA)

A supplementary instrument made in relation to an existing Will, explaining, altering, adding to, or qualifying its dispositions. It is deemed to be part of the original Will.

Execution Formalities: Must be signed and attested by two witnesses in the exact same manner as the original Will.
Revocation of a Will

• Subsequent Will: Executing a new Will containing a specific revocation clause.
• Physical Destruction: Burning, tearing, or destroying with the intention of revoking.
• Marriage Effect (Sec 69 ISA): Revokes a Will automatically for non-Hindus. (Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs, and Jains are exempt from this rule).

Comparison: Probate vs. Letters of Administration vs. Succession Certificate

Instrument Granted To Condition / Scope Compulsory Jurisdiction
Probate Only to an Executor named in the Will Certified copy under court seal proving validity of Will and executor's legal title. Mandatory for Hindu Wills executed in or covering immovable property in Mumbai, Kolkata & Chennai (Sec 57 & 213 ISA). Optional elsewhere.
Letters of Administration (LoA) To an Administrator (usually legal heir) Granted when no executor is appointed in Will, or executor dies/renounces, or in cases of complete intestacy. Requires court petition and testamentary citations to all legal heirs.
Succession Certificate To legal heirs of an intestate deceased Strictly for realizing debts and movable securities (bank balances, shares, mutual funds, FDs). Cannot be granted for immovable property. Issued under Sections 370–390 of ISA by Civil Court.
Section 15.3 • Lifetime Transfers
⭐ Tax Crucial

Gifts & Section 56(2)(x) Income Tax Provisions

Under Section 122 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (TPA), a Gift is the voluntary transfer of existing movable or immovable property made without consideration by a donor to a donee and accepted by the donee during the lifetime of the donor.

Execution Formalities (Section 123 TPA)
  • • Immovable Property: Transfer MUST be effected by a registered instrument signed by the donor and attested by at least two witnesses. Stamp duty is mandatory.
  • • Movable Property: May be effected either by a registered instrument or by mere physical delivery of the asset.
Section 56(2)(x) Relative Exemption

Gifts received from "Relatives" are 100% EXEMPT from income tax in the hands of the donee without any upper monetary threshold.

Other Exemptions: Occasion of marriage, under a Will, by way of inheritance, or in contemplation of death.
Statutory Scope of "Relative" under Section 56(2)(x):

(i) Spouse of the individual; (ii) Brother or sister of the individual; (iii) Brother or sister of the spouse; (iv) Brother or sister of either of the parents; (v) Any lineal ascendant or descendant of the individual; (vi) Any lineal ascendant or descendant of the spouse; (vii) Spouse of any of the persons mentioned in (ii) to (vi).

Clubbing Alert (Section 64): While gift to spouse is tax-free at transfer, any subsequent income generated by the gifted asset is clubbed back into the income of the donor spouse!
Section 15.4 • Co-Ownership Modes
Property Law

Joint Holding: Joint Tenancy vs. Tenancy in Common

Joint Tenancy With Survivorship

Co-owners hold an undivided interest with the Right of Survivorship (Jus Accrescendi). Upon the demise of one joint tenant, their entire interest automatically vests in the surviving joint tenant(s). It does NOT pass to the deceased's heirs or under their Will.

Common in financial accounts: "Either or Survivor" (E or S) or "Former or Survivor" (F or S).
Tenancy in Common No Survivorship

Co-owners hold distinct, specified, undivided fractional shares (e.g. 50:50 or 60:40). There is NO right of survivorship. Upon the death of a co-owner, their fractional share devolves to their legal heirs (by Will or intestacy), NOT to the surviving co-owner.

Default presumption in Indian law for immovable properties bought jointly unless explicitly drafted as joint tenancy.
Section 15.5 • Judicial Precedents
⭐ ULTRA HIGH YIELD • FREQUENTLY TESTED

Nomination vs. Legal Ownership

Perhaps the most heavily litigated and tested concept in Indian estate planning is the legal position of a Nominee. The Supreme Court of India has firmly established across decades that:

The Golden Legal Rule:

"A nominee is merely a statutory trustee / custodian appointed to receive the funds from the financial institution and give a valid discharge. Nomination does NOT confer beneficial ownership or defeat succession laws."

Landmark Supreme Court Jurisprudence:

1. Sarbati Devi v. Usha Devi (1984)

The Supreme Court held that the nomination under Section 39 of the Insurance Act does not make the nominee the owner of the insurance proceeds. The money forms part of the estate and belongs to the legal heirs.

2. Ram Chander Talwar (2010)

Reaffirmed that Section 45ZA of the Banking Regulation Act does not create third-party beneficial ownership. The bank nominee holds the deposit proceeds strictly in trust for all the legal heirs.

3. Shakti Yezdani (SC Dec 2023) ⭐

Settled conflicts regarding company shares: Supreme Court held that nomination under Section 72 of the Companies Act, 2013 does NOT override the laws of succession. Shares belong to legal heirs under Will/intestacy.

The Statutory Exception: "Beneficial Nominee" in Life Insurance (2015 Amendment)

By virtue of the Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015 amending Section 39 of the Insurance Act, 1938: If the nominee is an immediate family member—namely the spouse, children, or parents of the policyholder—such nominee is designated as a "Beneficial Nominee" and is legally entitled to hold the proceeds beneficially as the absolute owner, to the exclusion of other legal heirs!

Section 15.6 • Dispute Resolution
Family Law

Family Settlements & Arrangements

A Family Settlement (or Family Arrangement) is an agreement reached among family members to amicably resolve, avoid, or compromise existing or prospective disputes concerning joint family property, preserving family tranquility and honor without protracted civil court litigation.

Essential Legal Requisites:
  • • Must be bona fide and entered into voluntarily to resolve family disputes.
  • • Must involve members having antecedent title, claim, or interest in the estate.
  • • Must be fair, equitable, and free from fraud or undue influence.
Registration & Stamp Duty:
  • • Memorandum of Past Oral Settlement: If the document merely records a prior oral partition, it does NOT require registration.
  • • Contemporaneous Deed: If the instrument itself creates, declares, or assigns title in immovable property, registration and stamp duty are compulsory.
Section 15.7 • Trust Architecture & Taxation
⭐ ULTRA HIGH YIELD • TAXATION TESTED

Private Family Trusts: Architecture, Classification & Taxation

Governed by the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, a Private Family Trust is an obligation annexed to the ownership of property, arising out of a confidence reposed in and accepted by the owner (Trustee) for the benefit of another (Beneficiary) and the owner (Settlor).

1
Settlor / Author

Creates the trust and contributes initial corpus.

2
Trustee(s)

Holds legal title and manages assets fiduciary.

3
Beneficiaries

Holds beneficial / equitable interest and receives payouts.

4
Protector (Optional)

Supervisory oversight; power to veto or replace trustees.

Trust Category Key Characteristics Asset Protection & Ring-Fencing Taxation Framework (Income Tax Act)
Revocable Trust Settlor retains power to revoke trust and reclaim assets. Zero protection. Can be attached by settlor's creditors. Clubbed in Settlor's hands under Sections 61–63. No tax shield.
Irrevocable Determinate (Specific) Settlor divests all rights. Beneficiaries & their percentage shares are fixed in deed. Strong ring-fencing against future creditors of settlor. Taxed at the individual slab rates of beneficiaries (Trustee assessed under Sec 161 in like manner and to same extent).
Irrevocable Discretionary ⭐ Trustees hold full discretion over when and how much to distribute. Shares are indeterminate. Maximum asset protection; creditors of beneficiaries cannot attach trust assets since no fixed share exists. Taxed at the Maximum Marginal Rate (MMR) under Section 164.
The 3 Statutory Exceptions where Discretionary Trust is NOT taxed at MMR:

Under the proviso to Section 164(1) of the Income Tax Act, a Discretionary Trust is taxed at the normal applicable slab rate (not MMR) in the following 3 situations:

Exception 1 Where none of the beneficiaries has any other taxable income exceeding the basic exemption limit and is not a beneficiary in any other trust.
Exception 2 Where the trust is created by a Will exclusively for the benefit of any relative dependent on the testator for support and maintenance.
Exception 3 Where the trust is created exclusively for the benefit of a dependent relative who suffers from disability under Section 80U.
Section 15.8 • Incapacity & Vulnerable Heirs
Guardianship

Powers of Attorney (POA) & Guardianship

Powers of Attorney (POA Act, 1882)

• General Power of Attorney (GPA): Confers wide general authority to act in multiple matters.
• Special Power of Attorney (SPA): Restricted to a specific act (e.g. executing a single registry deed).
• ⭐ Automatic Termination: POA automatically terminates upon the death, mental incapacity, or bankruptcy of the principal.
• Suraj Lamp (SC 2012): GPA does NOT transfer ownership title of immovable property.

Guardianship Frameworks

• Natural Guardian: Father and Mother are equal natural guardians of a minor under Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956.
• Testamentary Guardian: Appointed through a Will to manage person/property of minor child post-demise.
• National Trust Act, 1999: Provides legal guardianship for persons with Autism, Cerebral Palsy, Mental Retardation, and Multiple Disabilities via Local Level Committees.
