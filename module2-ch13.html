Section 13.1 • Core Legal Architecture ⭐
SCRA Sec 2(ac)

Definition of Derivatives under SCRA, 1956

Statutory foundation and legal classification of derivative contracts in India

⚡ Fast-Track Refresher: Conceptual Definition

A derivative is a financial instrument whose value is not inherent but is derived from the price, value, or index of an underlying asset, rate, or reference index. Economically, derivatives are zero-sum contracts (excluding transaction costs and taxes), meaning the cumulative monetary gain of one party precisely equals the cumulative monetary loss of the opposing counterparty.

Section 2(ac) of Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 Must Memorize

Following the Securities Laws (Amendment) Act, 1999, derivatives were formally recognized as "securities" under Section 2(h) of SCRA. Section 2(ac) defines a "derivative" to include:

Clause (A)

A security derived from a debt instrument, share, loan, whether secured or unsecured, risk instrument or contract for differences or any other form of security.

Clause (B)

A contract which derives its value from the prices, or index of prices, of underlying securities. (Encompasses stock futures, stock options, index futures, and index options).

Clause (C)

Commodity derivatives (Brought under SEBI purview in 2015 following the merger of Forward Markets Commission with SEBI).

Clause (D)

Such other instruments as may be declared by the Central Government to be derivatives.

Milestones & High-Yield Regulatory Committees

Detailed mandates, structural recommendations, and the sequential launch timeline in India

⚡ Fast-Track Refresher: Global Derivatives Milestones

• 1730 (Dojima Rice Exchange, Osaka): Earliest recorded institutional forward exchange using rice bills.
• 1848 (CBOT): Chicago Board of Trade formed, pioneering standardized agricultural futures contracts.
• 1972 (CME - IMM): Chicago Mercantile Exchange launched the first financial futures (Foreign Currency Futures).
• 1973 (CBOE): Chicago Board Options Exchange established the first standardized exchange-traded equity options market.
• 1982 (CME): Launch of S&P 500 Index Futures, initiating modern index derivative trading globally.

Dr. L.C. Gupta Committee Appointed Nov 1996 • Submitted March 1998
Regulatory Policy

Appointed by SEBI under the chairmanship of Dr. L. C. Gupta to develop a robust regulatory framework for introducing exchange-traded derivatives in India.

SCRA Legislative Amendment: Recommended amending SCRA to explicitly define derivatives as "securities" (Section 2(ac)).
Phased Sequential Rollout: Explicitly mandated introducing Equity Index Futures first to mitigate manipulation risks, before permitting options and single-stock derivatives.
Independent Clearing Corporation: Mandated a separate, autonomous clearing corporation (or distinct governing council) to guarantee settlements.
Rigorous Membership Norms: Recommended strict capital adequacy and mandatory certification for trading/clearing members and sales staff.
Key takeaway: L.C. Gupta = Broad Policy & Regulatory Roadmap
Prof. J.R. Varma Committee Appointed May 1998 • Submitted 1998
Risk Containment

Appointed by SEBI to formulate the comprehensive operational risk containment architecture, margining methodology, and real-time surveillance standards.

SPAN Portfolio Margining: Recommended CME's Standard Portfolio Analysis of Risk (SPAN) to calculate risk on a whole-portfolio basis.
99% 1-Day Value-at-Risk (VaR): Mandated that initial margins must cover the worst-case loss scenario at a 99% statistical confidence level over a 1-day horizon.
Daily Mark-to-Market (MTM): Prescribed mandatory daily cash settlement of MTM debits and credits between clearing members and CC.
Online Real-Time Surveillance: Mandated continuous intraday monitoring of broker exposure and automated risk de-activation triggers.
Key takeaway: J.R. Varma = Mathematical Risk, SPAN & Margins

Section 13.3 • Instrument Taxonomy
Core Instruments

Derivative Product Types: Forwards, Futures, Options & Swaps

Symmetric obligations versus asymmetric rights, payoffs, and contract parameters

1. Forward Contracts OTC Bilateral

A privately negotiated contract between two counterparties to buy or sell an asset at a predetermined forward price on a future specified delivery date.

  • Symmetric Payoff: Both parties have mutual obligations; neither can walk away.
  • Counterparty Risk: Uncollateralized forward contracts carry high default risk.
  • Illiquidity: Difficult to cancel or unwind prior to maturity without counterparty consent.
2. Futures Contracts Exchange Traded

Standardized forward contracts legally traded on organized exchanges, backed by a central clearing corporation that guarantees settlement.

  • Standardization: Lot size, tick size, and monthly expiry dates are strictly fixed by the exchange.
  • Zero Counterparty Default: CC acts as counterparty via novation.
  • Daily MTM: Gains and losses are calculated and settled in cash every single trading day.
3. Options Contracts Asymmetric Payoff

Contracts granting the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy (Call) or sell (Put) the underlying asset at an agreed Strike Price on or before expiry.

  • Option Buyer: Pays upfront premium. Maximum loss = Premium paid; Potential profit = Unlimited (Call) or Substantial (Put).
  • Option Seller (Writer): Collects premium. Maximum profit = Premium received; Potential loss = Unlimited (Call) or Substantial (Put).
  • Exercise Style in India: All Indian stock and index options are European Style (exercisable only at expiration).
4. Financial Swaps Cash Flow Exchange

Private bilateral agreements between two institutions to exchange a series of periodic future cash flows over an agreed horizon based on a specified notional principal.

  • Interest Rate Swap (IRS): One party swaps fixed interest rate cash flows for floating rate cash flows (e.g., benchmarked to MIBOR).
  • Currency Swaps: Exchange of principal and interest payments in two different foreign currencies.

Market Architecture

Over-the-Counter (OTC) vs. Exchange-Traded Derivatives (ETD)

Clearing corporation novation, counterparty risk elimination, and institutional mechanics

Cornerstone Concept: Legal Novation Core Exam Favorite

In an exchange-traded market, once an order is matched between Buyer A and Seller B, the central Clearing Corporation (e.g., NSE Clearing Limited - NCL or Indian Clearing Corporation Limited - ICCL) steps in through the legal mechanism of Novation.

To the Buyer:

The Clearing Corporation becomes the legal Seller, guaranteeing delivery or cash settlement even if the original seller defaults.

To the Seller:

The Clearing Corporation becomes the legal Buyer, guaranteeing payment of funds even if the original buyer defaults.

Novation severs the direct bilateral contract and completely insulates market participants from counterparty default risk.

Comprehensive Comparison: OTC vs. ETD

Dimension Over-the-Counter (OTC) Derivatives Exchange-Traded Derivatives (ETD)
Contract Structure Customized to exact dates, sizes, and underlying terms desired by counterparties. Rigidly standardized lot sizes, expiry dates, and strike intervals.
Counterparty Risk High credit risk; dependent on counterparty solvency and goodwill. Virtually Zero; Clearing Corporation guarantees settlement via legal novation.
Price Transparency Opaque; prices negotiated privately between institutions without public tape. Highly transparent; real-time anonymous order book displayed publicly.
Collateral / Margining Varies bilaterally based on ISDA master agreements; not uniform. Mandatory upfront SPAN margin + Extreme Loss Margin (ELM).
Settlement & MTM Usually settled upon maturity date; no mandatory daily cash MTM. Compulsory daily Mark-to-Market (MTM) cash settlement after market close.
Market Liquidity Illiquid; contracts are usually held to maturity or terminated privately. Extremely liquid; participants can enter or square off instantly during hours.
Regulatory Oversight Lightly regulated or subject to inter-bank guidelines (e.g., RBI for currency/IR swaps). Comprehensively regulated by SEBI with strict position limits and surveillance.

Economic Roles

Market Participants: Hedgers, Speculators & Arbitrageurs

Economic motives, behavioral mechanics, and mathematical practical examples

Hedgers Risk Neutralizers

Hold an existing physical exposure in the underlying spot market. They enter derivatives exclusively to lock in prices and neutralize adverse price volatility.

Practical Example:

A mutual fund holding ₹50 Crore in equities expects a sharp market correction. The fund manager sells Nifty Futures to insulate the portfolio from systematic decline.

Objective: Minimize or eliminate risk, not maximize profit.
Speculators Risk Absorbers

Have no commercial or physical interest in the underlying asset. They intentionally absorb price risk in anticipation of directional moves to generate outsized leveraged gains.

Crucial Economic Function:

Speculators provide necessary market liquidity. Without speculators willing to take on price risk, hedgers would find no counterparties for their trades.

Objective: Generate high returns on margin capital by taking risk.
Arbitrageurs Price Harmonizers

Simultaneously enter opposite transactions in two related markets to capture riskless pricing anomalies between the spot market and derivatives market.

Crucial Economic Function:

Arbitrageurs enforce market efficiency and ensure the Law of One Price holds by bringing futures prices back to their theoretical cost-of-carry.

Objective: Lock in risk-free profit above the risk-free rate of return.
Step-by-Step Numerical Example: Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage Exam Calculation
Market Observations:

• Spot Price ($S_0$) of ABC Ltd = ₹1,000

• 1-Month Futures Price ($F_0$) = ₹1,015

• Financing Rate (Borrowing Cost $r$) = 6% per annum (0.5% for 1 month)

• Dividend Expected = Nil

Theoretical Fair Value Calculation:
Fair Futures Price = Spot × [1 + (r × t)]
Fair Price = 1,000 × [1 + 0.005] = ₹1,005

Futures is overpriced (₹1,015 actual vs ₹1,005 fair value).

Arbitrage Execution Strategy:
Step 1: Borrow ₹1,000 for 1 month at 6% p.a.
Step 2: Buy 1 share in Spot market at ₹1,000.
Step 3: Sell 1 share in Futures market at ₹1,015.
At Expiry: Deliver the spot share to settle the futures short position at ₹1,015. Repay borrowed principal + interest = ₹1,000 + ₹5 = ₹1,005.
Net Risk-Free Arbitrage Profit: ₹1,015 - ₹1,005 = ₹10 per share.

SEBI Risk Controls

Contract Sizing, MWPL & F&O Ban Mechanics

SEBI prudential risk management guidelines and position limits

Minimum Contract Value Band SEBI Norm

To discourage non-serious retail speculation and ensure derivative participants possess adequate risk-absorption capital, SEBI mandates that the minimum notional contract value must fall within:

₹15 Lakhs to ₹20 Lakhs

Lot Size Mechanism: The exchange adjusts the lot size of each underlying stock such that:

Contract Value = Lot Size × Underlying Stock Price

If a stock trades at ₹500, a lot size of 3,500 shares creates a contract value of ₹17.5 Lakhs (fitting squarely within the ₹15L–₹20L corridor).

Market Wide Position Limit (MWPL) Systemic Ceiling

To prevent market cornering, price rigging, and unnatural squeezes in single-stock derivatives, SEBI enforces an overall limit on open positions:

MWPL = 20% of Non-Promoter Free-Float Shares
Numerical Illustration:

• Total Issued Capital of XYZ Ltd = 100 Crore shares

• Promoter Holding = 60% (60 Crore shares)

• Non-Promoter Free-Float = 40% (40 Crore shares)

• MWPL = 20% of 40 Crore = 8 Crore shares (open across all F&O contracts).

The F&O Ban Period Mechanics (Highly Tested in NISM) Critical Rule
Ban Entry Trigger: ≥ 95% of MWPL

When the aggregate open interest (across all futures and options expiries) of a single stock crosses 95% of its MWPL, the stock immediately enters the F&O Ban period for the next trading day.

Restriction: No participant can open any fresh position. Trading members/clients can ONLY square off or reduce existing positions.

Ban Exit Trigger: < 80% of MWPL

The stock remains locked in the ban until position squaring off causes the aggregated open interest to fall below 80% of MWPL.

Restoration: Once OI drops below 80%, the exchange lifts the ban, and normal trading with fresh position creation is reinstated.

Penalties: Any market participant who increases open positions while a stock is under the F&O ban is subject to stringent financial penalties levied by the clearing corporation and exchange.

Tiered Position Limits Summary

Level Prescribed Regulatory Position Limit Purpose
Market Wide (Stock) 20% of Non-Promoter Free-Float Shares Prevents systemic cornering of company float
Trading Member (Broker) 20% of the Market Wide Position Limit (MWPL) Avoids concentration risk at a single brokerage house
Client / Non-Promoter Higher of 1% of Free-Float Market Cap OR 5% of Open Interest Prevents individual wealthy speculators from driving stock manipulation
